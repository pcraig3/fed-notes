Digital Transformation Canada and the faster-horses future

Transformation means doing new things, not doing the same things faster.
By Paul Craig
17th November 2026

Introduction

If you are reading this, you are surely aware that Digital Transformation Canada (DTC) is nigh! Not even nigh, it is in fact ‘now’: this very moment. If you have a glass of water nearby, those ripples you see on the surface are the booming footsteps of DTC approaching. And not a moment too soon! For digital-modernizers wandering aimlessly in the federal government’s digital desert, DTC is a shimmering oasis just come into view: finally something to look forward to, maybe even the thing we wanted.

Maybe with strong leadership and a clear mandate, DTC can solve two decade’s worth of plummeting government technology rankings. Maybe DTC is poised to achieve what so many “I have left my senior position in the public service and now I have a lot of ideas about how to fix it” articles couldn’t. Maybe it can even eclipse the impact of Federal Field Notes — a newsletter I started before I left the public service (although which made it very difficult to stay). With all sincerity, I very much hope DTC succeeds and you should too.

While the exact scope of DTC’s responsibilities is still unknown, a clear priority is “making better use of emerging technologies such as AI” to “scale shared solutions across government”. Like DTC, AI is also an Exciting New Thing with transformative potential that the Canadian government is committed to. And although I have as much information as you about DTC (ie, what the acronym stands for and everything on this page), it is nevertheless a credible theory that a new organization with a strong mandate, relevant expertise, and new AI-informed approaches can haul the digital transformation agenda out of the ditch and get it moving again.

My personal experience with AI is largely positive, particularly in regards to software development. It speeds up the work, it is a good thought partner, and it gives pretty useful tax filing information. Around the time ChatGPT was released in November 2022, there was plenty of debate over whether this was a genuinely useful technology or a ‘fancy autocorrect’ that was just lying to you all the time. You don’t really hear that so much anymore. AI certainly makes mistakes but it has proven itself to be an extremely useful technology, particularly in the field of digital product development.

My view is that AI has huge potential to significantly speed up digital service delivery at the average workplace when used in a considered way. But the Government of Canada (GoC) is not the average workplace. In terms of digital service delivery, the GoC is 10 years behind the average workplace. It would be terrific if the GoC would take any lessons from how I built digital services 11 years ago as a public servant for the UK government.

There is certainly an abundance of opportunity here to modernize the Government of Canada’s technology practices and if AI is a way to get there, that’s all to the good. But my fear is that the GoC squanders what it calls “the transformative potential of AI” for minor gains in efficiency while keeping the majority of its ponderous and inefficient processes in place. I’m calling this the ‘faster-horses future’, where introducing AI to a truly abominable workflow improves it to the point where it is merely ‘very bad.’

In this article, I will explain what the ‘faster-horses future’ looks like and why it would be an enormous letdown if that is all Digital Transformation Canada can achieve — even if it does result in a modest improvement to the GoC’s digital service delivery.

“Faster horses”

The ‘faster horses’ concept comes from a quote we have no evidence Henry Ford ever said:

“If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses.”
— Henry Ford (allegedly)

Ford famously mass-produced cars at a price people could afford, creating a whole industry and revolutionizing the dominant method of personal transportation. Before Ford’s Model T, you could still buy cars, but they were artisanally-built and super expensive. Cars were impractical for the majority of people, so the most common means of personal transportation (other than walking) was the horse-drawn carriage.

Ford’s quote captures the insight that if you asked someone with a horse-drawn carriage what would make it better, they would likely suggest minor improvements — like new wheels or a faster horse. They probably would not describe a completely different transportation paradigm. A faster horse would have been a minor improvement for a single carriage-owner, whereas cars revolutionized society. Today, nobody advocates going back to horse-drawn carriages.

The self-stated mission of the Canadian Digital Service (which I was a part of) was to “change government to serve people better.” In some ways, we did ‘serve people better,’ but we absolutely did not ‘change government’. The formation of DTC presents a rare opportunity to truly ‘change government to serve people better.’ This article argues that DTC should focus on radically rethinking how the Government of Canada delivers services, and not settle on ‘faster-horses’ improvements to the way things work today.

Why is it so hard to make a website for the government?

I started this newsletter to answer a simple question: Why is it so hard to make a website for the government? It turns out this simple question leads to many complicated answers — all of my previous articles explore different aspects of the problem. But, having answered this question eight different times now, I’ll try and sum it up.

Perhaps the major issue is this: the Government of Canada is an organization in which adding rules is easy but removing them is hard.

Over decades, the IT development workflow in the federal government has accumulated a huge amount of rules, many of which exist long past when they are needed or even useful. Without a parallel process of rule-removal, we have ended up with runaway compliance processes which add enormous amounts of make-work to even the simplest deliverables, and nobody is in charge of pruning this massive administrative burden to keep it at a reasonable size.

Imagine if you decided that you could always put on more clothes but never take any off. You would go from being agile and limber in your jogging shorts and T-shirt to not being able to bend your knees or elbows as you kept adding layers.

Slow is safe slow

I have previously described how launching a site in a large government department required almost 40,000 words of compliance documentation to release a 4-page website. While that definitely seems like a lot, a reasonable person might argue that it is important to have safety procedures, otherwise you could end up with the whole country learning a new phrase like “Nationwide Ground Stop”. Maybe the compliance is there for a reason, and being slow is the price of being safe.

This is a nice theory, but it doesn’t hold up to scrutiny. You can’t just assume all compliance is useful. Being slow guarantees you will be slow, but it doesn’t guarantee you are being safe.

Part of what goes into the 40,000 words of compliance is explaining how the website works on the day you are writing it. For example: what your site does, what all the URLs are, who has access to the backend, how many pages you have, etc. Knowing these things is important, but the document is only accurate until anything changes. By the time someone references that document a year later, a lot of that stuff is different.

Imagine you were required to write a 5-page document on where your dog is in your house so that you could find him later. 12 hours later the information in this document is out of date: it describes something that is no longer true. If your dog runs away, the report doesn’t help you. Writing a document that is only true for a moment in time is a bad way to solve this problem: it takes a long time to write the document but only a short time for it to become outdated.

But I’m not saying there is no other way to solve this problem. What I’m saying is that we should find a better solution. How about buying an AirTag, a much better way of tracking down lost dogs? Instead of blindly following a time-consuming and ineffective process, why not imagine what a better one might look like?

So let’s imagine! Presently, the Government of Canada is larded up with laborious compliance processes. Luckily for us, AI is a labour-saving technology. Square peg in a square hole, right? Well, it depends how it is used. Personally, I am worried about a scenario in which AI scores some easy wins without any actual process improvement and nothing else changes. The buried treasure here is using the opportunity afforded by AI to introduce new agile processes in how government services are built, tested, and deployed. The fool’s gold is a surface-level AI integration which makes an onerous system work slightly faster — the ‘faster-horses future’.

The faster-horses future

Returning to my website example, my team ended up writing almost 40,000 words of compliance documentation to release a 4-page site. I didn’t think that was a particularly good use of time: I’ve launched many websites without creating NaNoWriMo submissions and they seem to be doing just fine. But it wasn’t up to us. At the end of the day, we didn’t have the authority to change this process so how we felt about it was irrelevant. If we refused, we wouldn’t be allowed to launch our website. So we did what we had to: we wrote the documentation.

What would have really helped us would have been a magic robot that could quickly generate tens of thousands of words of plausible-looking documentation in a professional workplace tone. This is a pretty good use case for AI:

“CoPilot, please generate a 10-page document so that the person assessing my website will be happy.”

With CoPilot on the team, coming up with 40,000 words of documentation would be way easier. This would have saved our team hours, even days. Even though a lot of the stuff we were producing wasn’t useful, it would have been faster. Given a 6-month process to release a 4-page website, we might have saved a week or so this way. That’s about 5% faster.

On the one hand, 5% is a measurable improvement — if you got a 5% raise right now, I doubt you would complain. On the other hand, you wouldn’t exactly be buying a second house. 5% is better than nothing, but this is not a transformative change to your lifestyle.

If you kept every antiquated paperwork-driven rule that exists today, and then introduced an AI which makes it faster to generate paperwork, you would expect slightly faster turnaround times from that. In this scenario, everyone keeps doing basically the same thing as before, but now AI helps you get 5-8% faster overall.

This is what the ‘faster-horses future’ looks like:

  • Your 2-year project now takes 22 months because you can spit out compliance documentation much faster.
  • You still have to produce the same volume of pointless slop but now you have an Enterprise Slop Generator.
  • Your CoPilot generates a document, and then someone else uses their CoPilot to summarize your document.

Why do I think this is realistic? Because it is the culture of the organization. Even when clear opportunities appear to review existing burdensome processes, the reflexive institutional behaviour is some version of ‘our rules are fine, we just need to follow them better.’ An unusually public example of this can be found in the Auditor General of Canada’s report on ArriveCAN, and CBSA’s response to it.

ArriveCAN and the ‘more rules’ reflex

In February 2024, the Auditor General of Canada published a “scathing” report about ArriveCAN. The majority of it is focused on a botched procurement process, but they also reveal how a bug in the code meant “more than 10,000 iOS users [were wrongly instructed] to quarantine for 14 days.” (page 20-21). Despite all the laborious documentation and manual testing used in the Government of Canada, somehow (🤔) a major bug was released that caused a huge inconvenience for ten thousand people.

The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) responded to this by saying they were required to ship updates at a speed too fast to test. “2 Fast 2 Test” might be a good title for a car chase movie, it is a very bad excuse for not testing your software.

The Vice‑President, Information, Science and Technology Branch, recognizes that, given the constantly evolving pandemic environment and the requirement for 177 releases in 36 months, testing documentation was insufficient during ArriveCAN development.
— page 30

ArriveCAN was a major pillar of the Canadian government’s response during a national emergency, and in that rapidly-changing environment they were pushing updates about once a week.

When I worked in the UK government in 2015, we were releasing updates to the UK’s national procurement platform — the Digital Marketplace — multiple times a day. You probably haven’t ever heard of the Digital Marketplace; that’s because we were never in the newspapers responding to questions about massive bugs we released. We weren’t shipping “too fast to test” — we designed a process that enabled us to release updates quickly.

Similarly, in the federal government the Canadian Digital Service (CDS) has been successful at delivering major software platforms — GC Forms and GC Notify — rapidly and in the open. However, CDS is the exception and not the rule.

The typical federal department looks a lot more like CBSA, and they are definitely not doing weekly releases. Not only should you be able to meet that expectation during a national emergency, you should be able to do it all the time. When describing what got in the way, CBSA’s response explicitly admits their testing process was too slow:

It was not feasible to complete all testing documentation as per existing procedures in this emergency environment.
— page 30

So CBSA is discovered to have released a major bug that slipped past their testing regimen. They then admit their testing regimen wasn’t able to catch it. This seems like the prime opportunity to rethink their approach to testing. Instead, we get this:

[T]he Information, Science and Technology Branch will review and update existing testing procedures to ensure control steps are introduced and documentation is complete before any system or application is released to production. [Emphasis mine]
— page 30

“Ensure control steps are introduced” sounds like adding steps to a release process that is already not keeping up. Why is the answer here ‘we will make testing slower,’ instead of ‘we need to speed up testing’?

Earlier I described the Government of Canada as an organization where adding rules is easy but removing them is hard. This is a clear example of that reflex in action. CBSA’s response is not about streamlining an outdated and inefficient testing process, it is about adding steps — more rules, not less. The implicit choice here is between 2 bad options: we can either have fast updates that aren’t tested properly or we can have slow updates that are rigorously tested. Even though every app on your phone has figured out how to do both at once, inside of CBSA that option is not even being considered.

This is a picture of an organization which is stuck in its patterns of thinking. Sure, if your testing process is working then why change it? But when it publicly fails, you might expect to see some introspection here. However, that is not what we see. Not even a “scathing” public report by the Auditor General and a spate of negative press attention is sufficient to upset a status quo whose entire justification is to prevent exactly this kind of outcome.

This is not the response of an organization capable of transforming itself. If it were, it would have happened already. Instead, introducing transformational changes requires new leadership, modern methods, and a clear mission. Herein lies the potential of DTC: it brings a lot of ‘new’ into a traditional organization. But perhaps the greatest impact the DTC can have is not what it can bring, but what it can get rid of.

Fewer rules, but better

For anyone who has worked in a technical capacity anywhere outside the Government of Canada, it is almost baffling how the GoC IT environment operates. It’s like stepping into the IT version of Black Creek Pioneer Village where everyone is acting like it’s 200 years ago. In some ways this is charming — who doesn’t like freshly-churned butter? — but if you moved from a modern house with central heating, plumbing, and electricity to a 19th-century farmhouse, you would see a lot of room for improvement. If someone told you it takes a week to get a handwritten note to the next town, you might reasonably observe that where you come from it’s possible to instantly send a message across the world on your smartphone.

But while there are lots of opportunities for improvement, it is extremely difficult to change internal processes. As I have detailed in the past, there are many oversight groups that own part of the responsibility for service delivery, but no one is responsible for the cumulative process. The security team cares about security, the architecture team cares about architecture, the privacy team cares about privacy. Each team can define their own regulatory obstacle course that project teams must comply with, but the overall burden of all these processes combined is not anyone’s responsibility. This is the missing piece: a team with the responsibility for making sure existing processes are reasonable and work together effectively.

Amanda Clarke, notable Canadian digital government scholar, recently released a memo pointing directly at this problem: “Dismantling Ottawa’s Costly Web of Rules: How to Build a Functioning Federal Accountability Regime”. In it, she puts forth many promising suggestions for reducing administrative overhead:

  • Freeze the pile of rules and start cutting them
  • Rebuild the centre with delivery fluency
  • Cut governance layers aggressively
  • Work in the open

All of these ideas are worth trying, but they won’t happen on their own. Someone needs to own this. This is where Digital Transformation Canada can be maximally useful, taking on the responsibility for improving existing workflows by removing processes rather than adding more. Not only is this an idea worth trying, it also aligns with another major government initiative.

As I am writing this, the government is rolling out a Consolidated Approvals Request (CAR): described as “a shorter single document, with earlier implementation planning and less cumbersome decision-making.” The idea is to consolidate various approvals documents into one shorter one so that the government can move faster on major projects. This is exactly the kind of change I am suggesting for the government’s IT processes. Why on earth should it be easier to build a hydroelectric plant than a website?

Conclusion

The government’s press release describes how DTC is being formed by merging various parts of government together.

“Digital Transformation Canada will bring together Shared Services Canada and selected functions from the Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat, Public Services and Procurement Canada, and [...] the Canadian Digital Service.”

This is a strong positive signal, as it signals an appetite towards merging together groups with fragmented responsibility into one organization with a holistic viewpoint. The inclusion of the Canadian Digital Service (CDS) is particularly encouraging, as they have modern skills in digital service delivery that are rare inside of the federal government.

The potential here is, ahem, transformational. A major obstacle has historically been that there is no coordinating mechanism inside of the government to get everyone on the same page. As I have previously observed, the Government of Canada has an (overly) federated structure which fragments responsibility to the point where no one team is formally accountable for org-wide outcomes. Setting up a centralized, accountable authority with the mandate to use AI as a fulcrum for internal process improvement is a promising starting point.

To be clear: we don’t need AI to introduce better internal processes, but neither did we need Donald Trump to introduce the Consolidated Approvals Request. In both cases though, the first thing clears the path for the second one. AI can be introduced to help write code, proactively catch security vulnerabilities, to enable automated testing — there is a lot of opportunity here. On the other hand, if all that happens is that AI makes it easier to write long, pointless documents, that would be a huge waste of potential.

Transformational change means changing how digital services are built, tested, and released. It means doing different things, not doing the same things faster.

Canadians deserve responsive, modern, reliable services, not faster horses.
